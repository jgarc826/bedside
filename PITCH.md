# Bedside — pitch script (90 seconds)

## The problem (20s)
Every doctor will tell a patient they have cancer, or that their baby has no heartbeat, or that they can't drive anymore. Medical schools know this matters, so they train it. They hire actors called standardized patients, book a simulation room, assign a faculty observer, and run a 20-minute encounter.

That costs a school roughly $100 to $200 per student per conversation. So students get one, maybe three, practice reps in four years. Then they do it for real, as residents, with no one watching.

## The product (30s)
Bedside is an AI standardized patient. You pick a scenario, you talk to the patient out loud, and they react like a real person.

The patient has a hidden emotional state. If you jump to the diagnosis without finding out what she already knows, she shuts down. If you use a euphemism like "the results weren't what we hoped," she doesn't understand you just told her she has cancer, and she'll ask if that means it's benign.

When you're done, you get a SPIKES scorecard. It's the framework every school already teaches. But instead of a rubric, it quotes your exact words back to you: here's what you said, here's what the patient heard, here's what to say instead.

## Why it's different (15s)
This isn't a chatbot wrapper. The hard part of breaking bad news isn't the opening line. It's what you do when the patient starts crying or asks "how long do I have." That's what Bedside tests, and that's what no one gets enough reps on.

## Business (15s)
We sell to medical schools. They already have a budget line for this. One standardized-patient encounter costs about $120. A Bedside session costs about four cents. Students get unlimited reps, faculty get a dashboard of where their cohort is struggling, and the school spends less.

We start with the 150 US MD programs. Then nursing, PA, and residency programs, which today get almost no formal training at all.

## Close (10s)
Nobody should have their first real conversation about death be their first conversation about death. Bedside.

# Likely judge questions

**"Isn't this just ChatGPT with a prompt?"**
The patient maintains a hidden trust and emotion state that moves based on specific communication behaviors, and the grader is built around the SPIKES protocol that schools already assess against. The value is in the scenario design and the feedback design, not the model.

**"Who pays?"**
Med schools, out of existing simulation budgets. Students use it free, which is our distribution. Schools pay for cohort analytics and faculty review.

**"Why won't schools just build this?"**
They won't. Sim centers are staffed by educators, not engineers, and they're already stretched. They buy tools.

**"What about safety or accuracy?"**
Bedside doesn't give medical advice. The scenario facts are written by us. The patient only knows what the student tells them. The grader evaluates communication, not clinical decisions.

**"What's the moat?"**
Scenario library, scoring calibrated against real faculty ratings, and institutional data. Every session makes the grader better.

**"Why now?"**
Step 2 CS, the national communication skills exam, was cancelled in 2021. There is no longer a standardized check on this skill. Schools are looking for one.

# Demo flow for the video (2 min)

1. Home screen. Three scenarios. Say: "Pick the one every student dreads."
2. Click the pancreatic cancer scenario. Linda's face fills the screen and she speaks first. Say: "No transcript. You're looking at her, like you would be."
3. Do it WRONG first, briefly: jump straight to "So the biopsy showed adenocarcinoma, we'll get you to oncology." Watch her face shut down: head turns away, eyes drop, mouth tightens. Toggle instructor view to show trust dropping and her inner thought.
4. Click Restart. Do it right: ask what she understands, warning shot, plain language, pause, name the emotion, next step. Show her face come back to you and trust climbing.
5. End conversation. Let the scorecard load. Point at a "fix" moment that quotes your own words.
6. Scroll to the cost line: this session vs. $120.
